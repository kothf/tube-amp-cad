    ⚡ Tube Amp CAD — Manual

    Everything the application does, how to use it, what every value means, and how the simulator gets its numbers. Hover any label in the application to see the same explanations as a hint.

    1. What it is

    Tube Amp CAD designs and simulates vacuum-tube amplifiers (and hybrid and solid-state ones) in the browser. You draw a schematic; a circuit simulator works out the operating point of every part and the waveforms with a signal applied; bench instruments show the result as a real oscilloscope, spectrum analyzer, Bode plotter and curve tracer would. Then the same file is laid out as a printed circuit board.

    • Nothing to install, no account, no server. Everything runs on your computer. The circuit is autosaved in the browser and can be saved as a JSON file.
    • Windows. The Circuit CAD (schematic editor) is the centre. From its Windows ▾ menu you open the Oscilloscope, Spectrum analyzer, Bode plotter, Curve tracer and Board layout, each in its own browser window, so they can be arranged across several monitors. The CAD broadcasts every simulation result; the other windows listen and update by themselves. Each instrument and the curve tracer has the same Schematic | Board switch at the left of its toolbar (Alt+S and Alt+B from any window).
    • Browsers. Chrome, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, Opera and Safari are tested. Firefox is not tested.
    Hints everywhere. A label with a dotted underline has an explanation: rest the pointer on it for a moment. Buttons show what they do in the same way.

    2. Your first amplifier in ten minutes

    A common-cathode 12AX7 stage, simulated and measured. Each step names exactly what to click.

    1. Start a sheet. File ▾ → New…, choose A4 landscape, give it a title, Create. You get an ISO drawing frame with a title block.
    2. Place the tube. Type 12AX7 in the palette's search box and press Enter, then click on the sheet. The tube appears as VL1.1, with its pin numbers.
    3. Place the resistors. Type 100k, Enter, click above the anode: that is the plate resistor. Type 1.5k and place it below the cathode, and 1M from the grid to ground (the grid leak). Space rotates a part while you place it.
    4. Bypass the cathode. Type 47u to get an electrolytic and place it across the cathode resistor.
    5. Power and ground. From Sources place a DC supply (set it to 250 V in the inspector), and from Ground & connectors the Ground symbols.
    6. Signal. Place a Signal generator (1 kHz, 0.1 Vpk) and a Capacitor (100n) from it to the grid.
    7. Wire it. Click a pin, click the corners, and finish on another pin or on a wire. A wire that ends on a wire joins it with a dot. W starts a wire anywhere; Esc stops.
    8. Read it. With Live on, the circuit simulates after every edit. Every net shows its DC voltage and every wire its current. Click the tube: the inspector shows Va, Vg1k, Ia, Pa, and with the signal on the plate swing, the gain and the THD.
    9. Look at the waveform. From Instruments place an Oscilloscope; wire CH1 to the grid and CH2 to the plate. Its screen is live on the schematic. Double-click it for the full oscilloscope.
    10. Frequency response. Windows ▾ → Bode plotter shows gain and phase from 1 Hz to 1 MHz with the −3 dB points.
    11. Check it. Click an empty spot: the inspector shows the circuit's Checks: anything beyond its ratings appears there in red.
    12. Save it. Ctrl+S.
    Shortcut: the start screen, shown when the CAD opens on an empty sheet (and at File ▾ → Gallery…), has New circuit…, Open file… and every example with a picture of its first sheet, grouped as tube, hybrid, transistor and 555 circuits; click one to open it. Clear its tick box and it shows only from the menu. File ▾ → Examples lists the same finished circuits, drawn from published sources: a good way to see what a complete design looks like.

    3.2 Palette

    • Groups: Passive, Transformers, Sources (DC supplies and AC sources), Ground & connectors, Semiconductors, Integrated circuits, Instruments, Document, Tubes. Click a group's title to fold it; the browser remembers which are open.
    • Search box. Finds parts by name; part numbers (2N3904, IRF540, 1N4742A, 125ESE, 373BX); tubes (EL84, 6N2P, 300B); and values: 47k offers a 47 kΩ resistor or pot, 100n a 100 nF capacitor, 47u an electrolytic, 5H a choke, 300V a supply. Enter picks the first match.
    • Click a part, then click the sheet. Shift-click places several. Esc stops placing.
    • The Tubes group shows the popular tubes first; the button under the list shows all 110.

    3.3 Inspector

    The right-hand panel shows whatever is selected.

    • One part: its designator, every value (typed with SI prefixes: 4.7k, 220n, 1M), then its operating point and, with a signal, a With signal section; buttons to rotate, mirror and delete. Values beyond a rating are shown in amber (above 85 %) or red (above 100 %), with the share of the rating on a second line.
    • Several parts: how many are selected.
    • Nothing selected: the Circuit summary: parts, wires and nets; the power balance (power from the supplies, into the speakers, in the tubes, transistors and resistors); the Checks; and Renumber designations.

    Hover any label for its explanation. Every field and readout is described in Parts reference and Readouts.

    3.4 Status bar

    Left: the simulation state: "Simulated in 300 ms · 2 ms window, 16 cycles to settle", a progress bar while running, warnings in amber and errors in red. Right: the pointer's coordinates, or what you are hovering (a wire's voltage, AC swing and current; a sheet connector's partners).

    3.5 Resizing panels

    Drag the edge of the palette, the inspector or the bill of materials to resize it; double-click the edge to reset it. The instrument windows' side panels work the same way.

    4. Drawing schematics

    4.1 Placing and editing parts

    • Move: drag a part; its wires follow. Drag a wire segment sideways to move it.
    • Select several: drag a box over empty space, or Shift+click. Ctrl+A selects everything.
    • Rotate Space (R works too), mirror M (left to right), duplicate D, delete Del. Rotate and mirror also work while placing, and Space rotates the parts you are dragging. The same commands are in Edit ▾ and on the right mouse button over a part or wire (a right-click while placing or wiring still cancels it).
    • Mirror works for every part that can rotate, so a complementary pair or a tube driven from the right is drawn without crossing wires. The oscilloscope mirrors too; its screen stays upright.
    • Copy and paste: Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V.
    • Undo Ctrl+Z, redo Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z.
    • Pan: hold Space and drag, or middle-drag (a quick tap of Space rotates instead). Zoom: the wheel, at the pointer.

    4.2 Wiring

    • Click a pin, click each corner, and finish on a pin or a wire. A wire ending on another wire joins it with a junction dot; wires that merely cross are not connected.
    • W (or Wire) starts a wire anywhere, not only at a pin.
    • Voltmeter probe: P (or Probe) turns the pointer into a probe. Point at a wire or a pin after a simulation: a card shows the node's DC voltage, its AC (rms, peak-to-peak and frequency) and a small scope trace of its waveform, and the node's wires glow yellow. Click to leave the probe on that node; it keeps reading through edits and new simulations, also after you go back to Select. Esc, the card's × or a click on empty sheet removes it.
    • Esc ends the wire you are drawing.

    4.3 Sheets, frames and connectors

    • File ▾ → New… starts a circuit on a sheet with an ISO 5457 frame, a reference grid (columns 1, 2, 3…, rows A, B, C…) and an ISO 7200 title block, from A4 to A1, landscape or portrait. Add sheet… adds further sheets.
    • Frames are locked so they are not moved by accident; unlock one in its inspector. The title block's fields (title, number, revision, author, approver, dates, status, language) are edited in the frame's inspector.
    • Sheet connectors with the same signal name are one net, on this sheet and on others. Each shows where its partners are by zone: "A3,5 B2,5,8", on several sheets "1/A3,5 2/C1,4". A name used five or more times (B+, a bias line) reads as a supply rail and shows no list. Hover a connector to see all its partners in the status bar.
    • Power rails (Sources → Power rail) are the one-pin arrow with a name and a voltage, "VCC 9 V": an ideal DC supply from the pin to ground, so neither the supply nor its ground is drawn. Every rail and sheet connector with one name is one net, so a rail can be placed at each part that needs it. Shown as replaces the voltage next to the arrow with your own text ("5V to 9V"); Checks reports rails of one net with different voltages.
    • A circuit with several sheets opens on sheet 1. The sheet list, or F twice, shows them all.

    4.4 Drawing standards

    • Symbols follow IEC 60617; drawings follow IEC 61082-1: labels to the left of vertical symbols and above horizontal ones.
    • Designations use the classic letter codes (as in ГОСТ 2.710): R resistor, C capacitor, L choke, T transformer, VL tube, VD diode, VT transistor, HL lamp/LED, SA switch, BA loudspeaker, G generator/source, P instrument. A dual tube's sections are VL1.1 and VL1.2.
    • Renumber designations (inspector, nothing selected) numbers every part in reading order, sheet by sheet.
    • Resistors carry their power rating inside the symbol per ГОСТ 2.728.
    • Tube pin numbers are drawn beside each terminal.

    5. Parts reference

    Every part in the palette, what it models, and every field in its inspector. (This section is built from the application's own part library and hint texts, so it always matches the version you run.)

    5.1 Transistors, MOSFETs, JFETs, diodes, regulators and op-amps

    • NPN: 2N2222A, 2N3904, BC107, BC547B, BD139, MPSA42, MJE340. PNP: 2N2907A, 2N3906, BC177, BC557B, BD140, MPSA92, MJE350.
    • Drivers and power output pairs: 2SD669A/2SB649A, 2SC4793/2SA1837, MJE15032/MJE15033, TIP41C/TIP42C, 2SC5200/2SA1943, MJL3281A/MJL1302A, MJL21194/MJL21193; in TO-3 MJ15003/MJ15004, MJ15024/MJ15025, 2N3055/MJ2955.
    • Temperature (every bipolar transistor's inspector, °C, 25 by default): the junction temperature the part is simulated at. IS follows SPICE's law with the band gap (0.67 eV germanium, 1.11 eV silicon), so VBE falls about 2 mV/°C; hFE rises as (T/298 K)^1.5; germanium leakage doubles every 10 °C. Set it to see how a fixed-bias germanium stage drifts on a warm stage or next to a tube: an AC128 on fixed bias (1.5 MΩ from −9 V, 4.7 kΩ collector load) sits at VCE 5.2 V at 0 °C, 2.9 V at 25 °C, and is saturated by 45 °C. The SPICE export carries the part's temperature (temp=, on SPICE's scale: the simulator's 25 °C is SPICE's nominal 27 °C, TNOM=27, the same thermal voltage), the temperature terms of the model (EG, XTI, XTB) and the leakage as a B source.
    • Anode temperature (a tube's inspector): the anode cools by radiation, so its temperature goes as the fourth root of its dissipation: about 400 °C at the rated Pa in a chassis at 40 °C, and from about twice the rating it passes 525 °C and glows red (red-plating). With a signal the average Pa over the cycle counts. The schematic draws a red-hot anode glowing on the tube's symbol and Checks warn that it will fail within hours. The anode takes a couple of minutes to get there (a time constant of 15 s for a 1 W tube, about 35 s for a 25 W one).
    • Germanium: USSR MP39B, MP40A, MP41A, MP42B, MP25B, MP26B (PNP), MP38A (NPN), GT402G/GT404G, P213B; Western AC127/AC128, AC187/AC188, OC71, NKT275, 2N404, AD149, AD161/AD162. USSR types are written in Latin letters (MP39B = МП39Б); the search finds them either way. The parts list has PNP, germanium (AC128), NPN, germanium (AC127) and PNP, germanium (USSR) (MP39B) under Semiconductors → Transistors; any NPN or PNP transistor's Type list has the rest under its Germanium heading. The models give germanium's low VBE (about 0.2 V at 1 mA), the datasheet's hFE, and its slow transit (fT 0.15–4 MHz) as a base-emitter capacitance, so the treble roll-off of the old parts shows. Leakage is modelled: each germanium part has a typical ICBO at 25 °C (AC128 4 µA, MP39B 5 µA, AD149 100 µA, P213B 300 µA) flowing from collector to base, which the transistor amplifies to about (hFE + 1)·ICBO; it doubles every 10 °C. The inspector shows it under Leakage, in amber when it makes up half of IC or more. Pinouts of germanium cans vary between makers and years: check the part you have.
    • N-MOSFETs: 2N7000, BS170, IRF510, IRF540, IRF820, IRF840, IRFP240, IRFP250, IRFP460, laterals 2SK1058 and ECX10N20, depletion DN2540 and LND150. P-MOSFETs: BS250, IRF9540, IRF9610, IRF9640, IRFP9240, laterals 2SJ162 and ECX10P20.
    • JFETs (SPICE's JFET: the square law with IDSS and pinch-off, gate junctions, capacitances). N-channel: J201, J113, J310, 2N5457, MPF102, 2SK170 (BL grade), LSK170B, 2SK30A (Y grade); P-channel: J175, 2N5460, 2SJ74 (BL). Values are the datasheets' typical IDSS and gfs; real parts spread several times either way, so leave room in the bias or select parts. The inspector shows Id against IDSS and warns when the gate conducts. TO-92 with each type's pin order (the Toshiba types put the gate in the middle; the board flags pinouts that differ between makers), SOT-23 D-S-G as SMD.
    • Voltage regulators (Integrated circuits › Regulators): adjustable LM317 (1.5 A), LM317L (100 mA), LM338 (5 A, for DC heaters), negative LM337; fixed 7805, 7809, 7812, 7815, 7824, 78L05, 78L12, negative 7905, 7912, 7915. Pins IN, OUT and ADJ or GND. The model has the error amplifier (output resistance a few milliohms), the reference (1.25 V between OUT and ADJ, 50 µA out of ADJ), the current limit, the dropout rising with current, the line regulation (so an LM317 rejects ripple by about 66 dB, 78 dB with 10 µF on ADJ) and the minimum load an LM317 needs (3.5 mA: without it the output creeps up). The inspector shows the output, the headroom, the current and the chip's dissipation; Checks flags dropout, too little load, a voltage beyond the rating and a chip that needs a heatsink. TO-220 or TO-92 with the maker's pin order, SOT-223 / DPAK / D²PAK / SOT-89 as SMD.
    • TL431 shunt reference: the cathode sinks whatever holds REF at 2.495 V above the anode (dynamic impedance 0.2 Ω, REF bias 2 µA, up to 36 V and 150 mA). REF tied to K is a 2.5 V zener; R1 from K to REF and R2 from REF to A set Vka = 2.495 V × (1 + R1/R2). TO-92 (REF-A-K), SOT-23 and SOT-89.
    • Op-amps (Integrated circuits in the palette): TL071, TL072 (dual) and TL074 (quad) with JFET inputs (3 MHz, 13 V/µs), LM741 (1 MHz, 0.5 V/µs), LM301A, which has no compensation of its own (a capacitor between its pins 1 and 8: 30 pF for unity gain, 1 MHz and 0.5 V/µs; 3 pF from a gain of 10 up), LM324 (quad, single supply: its inputs and output reach V−, its output stops 1.5 V below V+), and for audio NE5532 (dual bipolar, 10 MHz, drives 600 Ω), OPA134 / OPA2134 (FET input, 8 MHz, 20 V/µs) and LM4562 (dual bipolar, 55 MHz, 20 V/µs). Wire V+ and V− of every amplifier to the supply: ±15 V, or one supply and ground for the LM324. The model has the datasheet's open-loop gain, gain-bandwidth, slew rate, output swing, output resistance, short-circuit current (sourcing and sinking), input bias and supply current; offset voltage, noise and the input common-mode limits are not modelled. The amplifiers of a dual or quad are one package: name them DA1.1, DA1.2 … (as the sections of a dual triode) and the board gets one DIP-8 or DIP-14 with each amplifier on its own pins. The inspector shows the output, its current and swing (amber when it clips), the voltage between the inputs and the package's dissipation; the checks flag a supply above the type's maximum (32 or 36 V) or no supply.
    • Power amplifier ICs (Integrated circuits › Power amplifiers): LM386 (4–12 V, DIP-8): inside, 50 kΩ from each input to ground, the output biased at half the supply from pin 7 (bypass it with 10 µF), gain 20 with pins 1 and 8 open and about 200 with 10 µF between them; 0.65 W into 8 Ω from 9 V at clipping. TDA2050 (Pentawatt, ±4.5 to ±25 V, gain 16 or more) and LM3886 (TO-220-11, ±10 to ±42 V, gain 10 or more, 38 W into 8 Ω and 68 W into 4 Ω from ±28 V as the datasheet). They are the op-amp model with the datasheets' gain, bandwidth, slew rate, swing and current limit. The LM3886 needs 0.5 mA drawn from its mute pin (8), a resistor to V− (10 kΩ from −28 V): without it Checks says it is muted (the simulation plays regardless). Checks also flag the supply out of range and the package's dissipation over its rating (idle: the dissipation with signal is not added up yet). Board: DIP-8 (SOIC-8 as SMD), the TO-220s standing with staggered leads.
    • NE555 timer (Integrated circuits, pins numbered as on the chip): the 5k–5k–5k divider (CV at ⅔ VCC), the trigger and threshold comparators, the latch, reset below 0.7 V with a weak pull-up (an open RESET reads high), the output stage from TI's data (high VCC − 1.4 V less 3 Ω, sourcing only; low 8 Ω), the discharge transistor (12 Ω) and the chip's supply current. The latch switches at the instant a comparator crosses its level, found inside the time step, so the timing does not depend on the step. The inspector reads the output's frequency and duty cycle, or its level and load at rest. A free-running 555 (with no signal generator or mains to follow) sets the simulation's cycle itself: the run finds its period after power-on, however slow, and captures three or more whole cycles past the first, longer one, so the oscilloscope, the probe, the stickers (averages over the cycle, as a meter reads) and ∿ Animate all show it running. File ▾ → Examples has five 555 circuits (astable, flashing LED, metronome, car tachometer, simplest oscillator), each checked against its formula. On the board it gets a DIP-8 (SOIC-8 as SMD).
    • Diodes: standard rectifiers 1N4001, 1N4004, 1N4007 (1 A) and 1N5404, 1N5408 (3 A, DO-201AD); fast and ultrafast UF4007, UF5408, MUR160, MUR460, HER108, FR107; Schottky BAT85, 1N5817, 1N5819, 1N5822, SB1100; SiC Schottky C3D02060A, C3D04060A, C4D02120A (TO-220-2, no recovery); the 1N4148 signal diode. Each has its forward curve, its junction capacitance and its stored charge, so a standard rectifier keeps conducting backwards for a few microseconds at the end of each pulse (reverse recovery) and a fast one for its datasheet trr; Checks compares the peak reverse voltage with VRRM and the average current with the rating. Bridge rectifiers: DF04M, DF10M (DIP-4), W04G, W10G (round WOB), B250C2300-1500A (inline), GBU4J, GBU8K (GBU), KBU8K (KBU), KBPC2510, KBPC3510 (chassis, Faston). Zeners: the 1 W 1N4728A … 1N4764A series (3.3–100 V) and 5 W 1N5349B … 1N5388B (12–200 V). LEDs: red, yellow, green, blue.
    • Gas voltage regulators (Gas-discharge tubes): 0A2 (150 V), 0B2 (108 V), 0A3 (75 V), 0C3 (108 V), 0D3 (153 V). Feed one through a resistor from a supply above its striking voltage: it lights and holds its maintaining voltage, rising by its regulation over 5–30 or 5–40 mA. Below its striking voltage it stays dark; below about 2 mA it goes out, so a capacitor across it (more than the datasheet's 0.1 µF) makes it relax into a sawtooth oscillator, as the simulation shows. The inspector shows whether it is lit, its voltage and current; Checks warn when it does not strike or runs outside its range. Board: a B7G or octal socket, anode pin 5, cathode pin 2. Under ∿ Animate a lit regulator glows violet.
    • Neon lamps (Gas-discharge tubes › Indicators): NE-2 (0.5 mA), NE-2H (bright, 1.9 mA) and ИН-3. Dark until about 75–110 V across them, then they hold about 60 V plus a few kilohms per ampere; a neon lights either way round, so on AC it glows on both half-cycles. Put 330 kΩ in series on 230 V mains; with a capacitor across it, fed through a few megohms, it flashes (period RC·ln((E − Vm)/(E − Vs))). The envelope glows orange by its current, settled or under ∿ Animate, where a mains neon flickers dark at each zero crossing and a flasher blinks. Checks warn over twice the design current. Board: the bulb standing on its leads.
    • Magic eyes (Gas-discharge tubes › Indicators): 6E5 and 6Е5С (sharp cutoff: the shadow 90° with the grid at the cathode, shut at −8 V) and 6U5 (remote cutoff, shut at −22 V). Pins: target T, triode plate P (inside also the ray-control electrode), grid G, cathode K. Wire the target to about 250 V and the plate to the same supply through 1 MΩ; the grid takes the AGC of a radio or a rectified signal for a level meter. The triode is fitted to RCA's data (0.24 mA through 1 MΩ at no bias; checked against ngspice with the exported model) and the target draws its 4.5 mA. The green fan on the symbol opens and closes with the simulated grid, settled or under ∿ Animate; the inspector gives the shadow's angle. Board: a 6-pin (UX6) or octal socket, or wires to the front panel.
    • Output transformers (Transformers): single-ended and push-pull / ultralinear. Each has the impedances, the primary inductance (the bass), the leakage inductance and the winding capacitance (the treble and its resonance), the winding resistances, and a core that saturates: Rated power and Full power down to (plus the Max DC current of a single-ended one) set its knee, so at the rated power and that lowest frequency the core stands at 75 % of saturation. Below that frequency the power it can pass falls with the square of the frequency; past it the bass distorts and the primary current peaks, and a push-pull core with DC out of balance between its halves saturates sooner. The Model list fills in real transformers: Hammond 1608, 1609, 1615, 1620, 1645, 1650F–1650W and the 1750H guitar replacement (push-pull), 1627SEA–1640SEA (single-ended), from each drawing (inductance and leakage measured at 60 Hz, 10 V), and Edcor XSE, GXSE, CXSE, XPP, GXPP and CXPP parts, whose inductance, leakage and resistances are worked out from their published band and insertion loss (the inspector says which values those are). Change the secondary impedance for another tap; change anything else and the part shows your own values. The Hammond 125SE part keeps its taps and gets the same core and leakage. The inspector shows the bass and treble corners and, after a run, the core flux at its peak as a share of saturation; Checks warn when it saturates. The Bode plotter linearises the core at its operating point, so DC in a single-ended primary lowers the inductance the bass sees. SPICE export writes the windings with their leakage as coupling below 1; the saturation is not exported.
    • Interstage and input transformers (Transformers): a turns ratio 1:n, an optional centre-tapped secondary (SC) for push-pull grids, the primary inductance (the bass), the leakage inductance and the secondary's capacitance (the treble peak), winding resistances and the primary DC the core is gapped for (0: ungapped, Checks warn at any DC). Presets give typical values for a 1:1 and a 1:2 CT interstage, a microphone 1:10, a moving-coil step-up and a line input; type your transformer's datasheet values over them. Core saturation is not modelled.
    • Switches (Switches & relays): the changeover switch; the on–off switch, a toggle or a push button, normally open (it makes while pressed) or normally closed (it breaks); the rotary selector, one pole of 2 to 12 ways. Double-click one on the sheet to flip it, press it (it stays pressed until the next double-click) or turn it to the next way. Sections named SA1.1, SA1.2 … are one switch and move together: a DPDT, a two-pole selector. A closed contact is 10 mΩ, an open one 10 GΩ. On the board a push button is a 6 mm tactile switch; toggles and selectors sit on the panel, wired.
    • Relays (Switches & relays): Omron G5LE-1 (SPDT, 10 A at 250 V AC: mains, standby, speaker muting) and G5V-2 (DPDT, 2 A: input and channel switching), with a 5, 12 or 24 V coil. Pins as EN 50005 numbers them: coil A1–A2, common 11 (21), normally closed 12 (22), normally open 14 (24). The coil is its resistance (rated voltage² / coil power) and an inductance that brings its current up to the must-operate level in 60 % of the datasheet's operate time; the contacts close when that current reaches 75 % of the rated value and open below 10 % (G5LE-1) or 5 % (G5V-2). A diode across the coil (needed when a transistor drives it) slows the release, as on the bench: checked against ngspice, 6.00 ms to pull in and 9.97 ms to drop out for a G5LE-1 at 12 V. The schematic draws the contacts pulled in when the simulation has them so, and the inspector says which way they are. The cell's conductance goes as a power of the LED's current through the two datasheet points; it brightens in 2.5 ms and dims to 100 kΩ in 35 ms, so a tremolo's LFO through it throbs and a soft switch does not click.     • Phono cartridge (Sources): a moving magnet (typical, Ortofon 2M Red) or moving coil (high output, Denon DL-103) behind its coil's resistance and inductance, playing a tone at its rated output (rms at 1 kHz, 5 cm/s). A record cuts it with the RIAA curve (IEC 60098: −19.3 dB at 20 Hz, +19 dB at 20 kHz, with the 3.18 µs pole), so a correct RIAA preamp plays it flat: sweep it with the Bode plotter and the preamp's output should be level. Flat is the cartridge's level at every frequency, to measure the preamp's own curve. Load a moving magnet with 47 kΩ and 100–300 pF in all.     • Controlled sources (Sources): a voltage-controlled voltage source (SPICE E, a gain) and a voltage-controlled current source (SPICE G, a transconductance with an output resistance). Their control pins draw nothing. Use them for block diagrams and behavioural models: a G of a tube's gm with its ra in parallel is the tube's small-signal model.
    • Protection (Passive): fuse, 0.1–10 A, time-lag or fast-acting, 5 × 20 mm, 6.3 × 32 mm or TR5, simulated at its cold resistance; Checks warns from 75 % of its rating (the rms current). Inrush limiter (NTC), Ametherm SL10 5R003, SL22 10005, SL22 2R510, SL22 5R012, SL32 10015: the simulation finds the resistance it settles at, warmed by its own rms current (the maker's R = k·I^−n through its resistance at 100 % and 50 % of Imax), while the power-on transient starts it cold.     • Connectors (Ground & connectors): jack 6.35 mm mono switched (its SW contact rests on the tip until a plug goes in: double-click the jack to plug in), mono, stereo, 3.5 mm stereo and RCA; IEC C14 inlet (L, N, PE, on the chassis); terminal block 2–8 ways at 5.08 or 7.5 mm; pin header 1 × 2 … 1 × 10 at 2.54 mm; Faston tab 6.3 or 4.8 mm.     6. Simulation

    6.1 What is simulated

    Every run computes:

    1. The DC operating point: every node voltage and every part's current at rest.
    2. The steady state with the signal: the waveforms once every capacitor and choke has settled, over a window that is a whole number of signal periods (and of mains periods in a mains-fed circuit).

    The oscilloscope can also run the power-on transient: the first seconds after switch-on, with every capacitor empty.

    The Bode plotter runs a separate small-signal (AC) analysis in the background.

    6.2 Live and on demand

    • Live on: every edit triggers a run. A run that does not finish in a moment continues in the background; the inspector shows a spinning percentage until it is settled.
    • Live off: nothing runs until ▶ Simulate or Ctrl+Enter.
    • Either way, slow power supplies (large reservoir capacitors behind a choke) are run until fully settled. Press the button again to stop a long run.

    6.3 Mains-powered circuits

    Draw AC mains → power transformer → rectifier → reservoir capacitor → choke or resistor → filter capacitor, exactly as built. The simulator runs the rectifier cycle by cycle, so ripple, sag under load and the rectifier's peak currents are real. Voltages and currents on the schematic are averaged over the settled window, as a meter reads them.

    Faster drawing: while working on the audio stages, feed them from a DC supply (ideal DC) with an internal resistance; add the real supply when the stages are done.

    6.4 When a run fails

    • A missing ground: the checks report it; place a Ground symbol.
    • Unconnected pins: the checks name them. Every node also needs a DC path to ground: a grid fed only through a capacitor needs its grid leak.
    • A shorted winding is reported instead of stopping the solver.
    • It does not settle: a circuit that oscillates (a feedback loop with too little phase margin) never reaches a steady state. Look at the Bode plot for a peak, and at the power-on transient.

    6.5 Tolerance runs

    Real parts are not their nominal values: resistors are ±5 %, electrolytics ±20 %, and two tubes of one type can differ by 20 % or more in plate current. View ▾ → Tolerance runs opens the Tolerance tab under the sheet, which simulates the circuit once as drawn and then once per run with every part varied, and shows how far each value moves.

    • Runs: how many varied circuits (each settles fully, as ▶ Simulate does). 20 shows the spread; 100 or more shows the rare corner. The runs share all the computer's cores, so as many run at once as it has cores. A circuit slow to settle (a choke-input or valve-rectified supply, say) has its varied runs start from the circuit as drawn already settled, so each has only a short way to go; they settle just as fully.
    • R ±, C ±: the tolerance of every resistor and pot (both halves of a pot move together, so the wiper stays put) and of every capacitor and electrolytic.
    • Tubes ±: every tube draws that much more or less current than its model at the same voltages, as a stronger or weaker tube does. Rectifiers stay as they are.
    • Each value is normally distributed about its nominal with the tolerance as two standard deviations (about 95 % of parts are within it), and never beyond it. Seed: the same seed repeats the same runs.

    The table lists each tube's Ia, Va and Pa, each transistor's current and dissipation, each speaker's output and each scope channel's AC (Vrms): the circuit as drawn (Nominal), the Min, Mean and Max over the runs, the Spread (two standard deviations against the nominal) and in how many runs the part was over its rating (Pa and Va for a tube, Ic and Ptot for a transistor). Runs that do not settle are left out and counted. Press ▶ Run again to stop; an edit marks the table as the old circuit's.

    Reading it: a cathode-biased stage barely moves when its tube is changed; a fixed-bias output stage moves a lot. If any run takes a tube over its Pa, a real one of that type may too.

    6.6 Power sweep

    An amplifier is rated by the power it gives at a set distortion. View ▾ → Power sweep opens the Power tab under the sheet, which simulates the circuit at a row of input levels and plots the distortion against the output power.

    • Output: what is measured. A speaker gives its power (W); a scope channel gives its AC (Vrms), for a preamp or a stage without a load.
    • Input … to … dB: the quietest and loudest level, in dB from what the generator is set to now (0 dB). Every signal generator, guitar pickup and phono cartridge is scaled together, so a stereo amplifier's two channels stay driven alike; the mains stays as it is. Steps: how many levels, evenly spaced in dB.
    • The levels run at once on all the computer's cores, each settling fully as ▶ Simulate does. Where the distortion passes 1 %, 5 % or 10 % between two levels far apart (an output stage clips within a dB or two), the sweep adds three levels between them, twice, so the marks are read closely.

    The cards read the output at 1 %, 5 % and 10 % THD (interpolated between the two levels either side, on log scales) and the small-signal gain from the generator to the output. The chart has THD (cyan) and the 2nd (amber) and 3rd (violet) harmonics against the output, both on log scales, with dashed lines at 1 % and 10 % and a green line at the generator's level now. The table lists every level: the generator's output (rms), the output, THD (2nd to 10th harmonic) and the 2nd and 3rd harmonics in dBc. Hovering a point lights its line and the other way round; Use on a line sets the generator to that level (Ctrl+Z undoes it). Press ▶ Run again to stop; an edit marks the sweep as the old circuit's.

    Reading it: a single-ended triode stage has mostly 2nd harmonic that rises steadily with the level; a push-pull stage cancels the 2nd and shows the 3rd. A curve that runs flat and then turns up within a dB or two is an output stage clipping, or a feedback loop running out of gain.

    6.7 Listen

    Numbers tell a lot, but an amplifier is in the end judged by ear. View ▾ → Listen opens the Listen tab under the sheet: a recording is played through the simulated circuit and you hear its output, switching between the input and the output as it plays.

    • Play: what drives the circuit. Guitar riff is made here (plucked strings: a power chord, two single notes and an open chord), the same every time. The generator's tone is the generator's waveform at its frequency. Your file opens a WAV or MP3 file (Open file…, or drop it on the tab); FLAC, OGG and M4A work where the browser reads them. A stereo file drives the first generator with its left channel and the second with its right, so a stereo amplifier plays in stereo.
    • 70s rock radio (live) plays the live broadcast of RADIO BOB! 70s Rock (Germany, 128 kbps MP3). On ▶ Render the tab tunes in, catches the next Length seconds of whatever is on air (a song, a jingle or the presenter) and plays them through the circuit in stereo like a file; ↻ Tune in again catches a fresh piece and renders it. The circuit is simulated, not run in real time, so it is always a piece of the broadcast, not a continuous stream. It needs the internet; when the station cannot be reached, the tab says so.
    • From, Length: the excerpt of a file, up to 30 s. Level: 0 dB puts the recording's peak at the generator's amplitude (a pickup's EMF, a cartridge's output); raise it to drive the circuit into overload.
    • Hear: a speaker's voltage, two speakers as stereo, or a scope channel (for a preamp).
    • ▶ Render first lets the circuit settle with its generators silent, then plays the recording through it at 48 kHz, every sample a step of the simulation. The clip is cut into one piece per core, each started from the settled circuit with 0.4 s of the clip before it so its coupling capacitors and supply have caught up, and joined to the next with a 20 ms crossfade; with the mains in the circuit the pieces start on whole mains periods, so the hum runs on in phase. Joined, the pieces match one run of the whole clip to a millionth.

    ▶ Play plays the input and the output together; A · Input and B · Circuit switch between them at once, without losing the place. Match loudness plays the output as loud as the input, so the switch compares the sound and not the level; without it each plays at its own peak. Click a strip to play from there. Save WAV saves the output (16-bit, its peak at full scale). The note above the strips gives the output's peak and rms in volts. An edit marks the render as the old circuit's.

    Try: example 06 or 07 with the guitar riff at 0 dB and then at +12 dB: the first is clean, the second is the output stage clipping, the sound of a tube amp pushed hard. A/B against the input to hear how much of the treble the output transformer and the speaker load keep.

    6.8 Parameter sweep

    What does a larger anode resistor do to the gain, the distortion and the tube's dissipation? View ▾ → Parameter sweep opens the Sweep tab under the sheet, which steps one value of one part across a range, simulates the circuit at every step and shows how everything follows.

    • Part and Value: any part with a number to change, and which number: a resistor's resistance, a capacitor's capacitance, a pot's wiper position, a supply's voltage, a generator's frequency or amplitude, a transformer's ratio… The value it has now is shown next to it. Until you pick one, the first resistor is chosen, and the chart plots the speaker's output (else a scope channel).
    • From … to: the first and last value, typed as on a part (100k, 2.2u, 330); left empty, half and twice the value now (a wiper: end to end). Steps: how many values. Log spaces them in even ratios, as resistor values are; Linear in even steps.
    • The steps run at once on all the computer's cores, each settling fully as ▶ Simulate does. The circuit itself is left as it was.

    The table lists each tube's Ia, Va and Pa, each transistor's current and dissipation, each speaker's power and THD and each scope channel's AC and THD, with a small trend line over the steps, the value at the first and last step, the range, and in how many steps the part was over its rating. Click a line to plot it against the swept value: the chart marks the value now (dashed green) and the rating where there is one. A scope channel's line also overlays one cycle of its wave at every step (AC coupled), from amber at the first step to cyan at the last, so the waveform's change shows at a glance. The buttons above the table are the steps: hover one to light its point and wave, click one to set the part to that value (Ctrl+Z undoes it). An edit marks the sweep as the old circuit's.

    Try: example 03 with R2 (the anode resistor) from 47k to 470k, then click P1 CH1 THD: the distortion falls as the load line flattens, while VL1 Va drops towards the knee.

    6.9 Noise and hum

    How quiet is the amplifier? View ▾ → Noise and hum opens the Noise tab under the sheet. With the generators quiet, it works out what the amplifier puts out on its own: the hiss of its resistors and tubes, the supply's ripple, and the hum AC heaters would add.

    • Output: where it is measured: a speaker, or a scope channel (for a preamp).
    • Heaters: how the tubes' heaters are fed, for the heater hum. AC, one side grounded; AC, centre-tapped (or a humdinger); DC (no heater hum).
    • Tube 1/f corner: below this frequency a tube's noise rises as 1/f (flicker noise). Real tubes vary widely, from about 100 Hz to a few kHz, even between two of one type; 1 kHz is a middling guess, and 0 leaves it out.
    • Mains: 50 or 60 Hz, for the heater hum of a circuit with no mains part (with one, its frequency is used).

    Noise. Every part is linearized at its idle point (a mains-fed supply at its averaged steady state, as for the Bode plotter) and every noise source is carried to the output: each resistor's thermal noise (4kTR), each tube's (an equivalent noise resistance of 2.5/gm at its grid, a pentode's with its partition noise, (Ia/Ik)·(2.5/gm + 20·Ig2/gm²), and the 1/f rise), each transistor's shot noise and each FET's channel noise. The generator's output resistance is counted, as a real source's is; the speaker's own is not. Op-amps, regulators and the like are noiseless here. The cards read the output noise flat and A-weighted over 20 Hz – 20 kHz, the input noise (the output's over the gain at 1 kHz from the generator: what a noiseless amplifier would need at its input) and its density at 1 kHz in V/√Hz. The chart plots the noise density at the output (cyan) and carried back to the input (amber) from 10 Hz to 100 kHz. The Noise from table lists the parts the noise comes from, with each one's share of the A-weighted output noise.

    Hum. With the mains in the circuit, the circuit is simulated with the generators quiet and the output's hum read at every harmonic of the mains: a full-wave supply's ripple is at 100 Hz (120 Hz), a half-wave's at 50 Hz. A circuit fed from DC supplies has no ripple. The schematic does not draw heaters, so the AC heaters line is an estimate from typical figures: an indirectly heated cathode sees its heater through 5 pF and 100 MΩ of leakage, and the heater's average potential against it is half the heater voltage with one side grounded, about 5 % of that with a centre tap (the balance a real winding reaches; a trimmed humdinger does better). A directly heated tube's filament carries that same voltage in series with its cathode. 12.6 V heaters are taken as wired for 6.3 V. The hum lines are A-weighted too (50 Hz is −30 dB on the A scale, 100 Hz −19 dB).

    The main card is the signal-to-noise ratio, noise and hum together and A-weighted: re 1 W into the speaker's nominal impedance, or for a scope channel re the output the generator gives now. The hum table gives each line re the same.

    Reading it: in a power amplifier the first tube and the resistors at its grid and cathode make nearly all the hiss; a later stage's noise is divided by the gain before it. A large grid-leak or grid-stopper share means a lower value would help; a large first-tube share, a quieter tube (higher gm). 80 dB(A) re 1 W is ordinary, 90 dB(A) good, 100 dB(A) very quiet. Ripple at the speaker goes down with a larger reservoir or filter capacitor, a choke, or feedback.

    7. Measuring on the schematic

    • Voltages: each net carries a sticker with its DC voltage in yellow. A net with AC on it adds the AC in cyan as ~20.1Vrms (the rms of the wave about its mean, over the settled window); a net with no DC, such as the input or a signal after a coupling capacitor, shows the AC alone. The wires of the nets that carry the signal glow cyan, so the signal path stands out from the DC supply and bias nets. A rail with only some ripple on it (AC under 1 % of its DC) shows the ripple in the sticker but does not glow. Hovering a wire reads the same in the status bar: DC, AC in Vrms and Vpp.
    • ∿ Animate (toolbar, next to Simulate): plays one cycle of the signal slowed to 3 seconds. Dots run along each wire with its DC or its AC current, chosen by the wire's node voltage: where the node's DC voltage is at least its AC (rms), violet dots flow steadily in the DC current's direction, as in a plate's supply lead; elsewhere pale dots swing with the AC current, as at a grid, the mains, a rectifier's anodes or a speaker. A filter capacitor on a DC rail passes only the ripple, so it shows AC dots. Brighter dots carry more current. Each node's wires turn orange as it swings above its DC level and blue below, so the inversion through a stage, the signal returning through the supply and a bypass capacitor's work are all visible. Each wire and node is scaled to its own peak, so a grid's microamps move as clearly as a plate's milliamps. An LED's triangle fills with its colour, brighter with more current (full at 10 mA). A cycle of a quarter second or longer, such as a 555 flasher's, plays in real time, so its LED blinks as it would on the bench. A circuit with LEDs but no cycle at all (no signal generator, mains or free-running 555) plays its first 4 seconds after power-on instead, in real time, over and over. Click again to stop.
    • LEDs also light with their simulated current when the animation is off: by the average current, dark when none flows.
    • Legend: a key in the bottom-left corner of the sheet explains the marks that are showing (the signal glow, the DC and AC stickers, the currents). It fades while the pointer is over it, so the drawing under it shows. View ▾ → Legend hides it; the browser remembers.
    • Currents: each run of wire carries its DC current and direction ("↓1.38mA"). Currents come from every part's terminal currents and are passed along the wiring, so a junction shows how the current splits.
    • Stickers go where they cover no text, symbol or other sticker; if there is no room, a sticker is left out. Toggle them in View ▾.
    • Hover a wire: its voltage, AC swing and current appear in the status bar.
    • Oscilloscope parts show their two channels live on the schematic.

    9. Checks and ratings

    With nothing selected, the inspector's Checks list every problem in the circuit. Each tube's inspector shows its values as a share of their ratings: amber above 85 %, red above 100 %.

    9.1 Circuit checks

    • Unconnected pins, shorted parts, a missing ground, sheet connectors without a partner.
    • Resistor dissipation against its power rating (rate a resistor at about twice what it dissipates).
    • Output-transformer primary DC current against its rating (single-ended cores saturate above it), and an output transformer's core driven into saturation (its flux over the run past 95 % of the knee).
    • Transistor Vce and dissipation; zener power.
    • Capacitors against their voltage rating (a warning above 80 %, an error over it) and a reversed electrolytic.
    • Diodes and bridges: the peak reverse voltage over the capture against VRRM, the average current against the rating.
    • Regulators, TL431, op-amps and power amplifier ICs: supply voltage too high or too low, dropout, minimum load, dissipation for the package, a muted LM3886.
    • Fuses (above 75 % of the rating they age, over it they blow), varistors over their continuous voltage, NTC limiters, relay coils that may not pull in or overheat, LEDs over 40 mA in a vactrol.
    • Neon lamps and gas regulators that do not strike, or carry less than they need or more than their rating; a magic eye's target over its voltage.
    • Interstage and input transformers: DC in an ungapped primary (feed it through a capacitor), or more than the gap allows.
    • Power rails of one name with different voltages.

    10. Instruments

    All instruments read an Oscilloscope part in the circuit: place one and wire its CH1 and CH2 where you want to measure and its COM to the reference (usually ground). With several scope parts, choose which one in the window's Scope part list. The scope part's inspector has Oscilloscope ↗, Spectrum ↗, Bode ↗ buttons that open the window on that part.

    10.1 Oscilloscope

    • Two channels, DC or AC coupled; 10× (10 MΩ) or 1× (1 MΩ) probes that load the circuit like real ones.
    • Auto volts/div with offset; the timebase locks to the measured frequency; edge trigger on either channel, rising or falling; X-Y mode.
    • Scale labels on every axis: time below, CH1's volts left, CH2's right, on round values that follow zoom and position.
    • Readouts: Vpp, Vrms, dBV and DC per channel, and the CH2/CH1 gain and phase.
    • Channels with no probe wired are greyed out.
    • Analysis: Steady state (the settled circuit, one window repeated as a triggered scope shows it) or Power-on (the first 0.1–2 s after switch-on, with an overview strip of the whole record).
    • Autoset resets the view.

    10.2 Spectrum analyzer

    • The FFT covers an exact whole number of signal cycles, so there is no windowing error and no leakage.
    • Harmonic table H2…H10 with frequency, level and percentage, and the THD.
    • Other components: anything that is not a harmonic: mains hum and its harmonics, intermodulation products.
    • Levels in dBc (relative to the fundamental: harmonics read directly as distortion) or dBV (absolute).
    • Linear or logarithmic frequency axis: on the log axis 50/100 Hz ripple and the audio harmonics read in one view.
    Reading distortion: a single-ended stage makes mostly H2 (asymmetric); a balanced push-pull cancels the even harmonics and leaves H3. A rising H3, H5, H7 series means symmetrical clipping.

    10.3 Bode plotter

    • Gain in dB and phase from 1 Hz to 1 MHz at the scope part's channels; or, in Loop gain mode, the gain around a feedback loop with its phase and gain margins (below).
    • Traces: CH1, CH2, or CH2 ÷ CH1, the gain between the two probes (one stage of a chain).
    • Gain: re the generator (absolute), or re 1 kHz (normalised, to read the shape).
    • Response table: gain and phase at 1 kHz; the −3 dB points either side; the level at 20 Hz and 20 kHz; any peak above the 1 kHz gain (an output transformer's leakage resonance, a feedback loop short of stability).
    • Generator: which signal generator drives the sweep; the others are switched off.
    • It is the small-signal response: every part linearised at its idle operating point (a mains-fed supply at its averaged steady state). Large-signal effects (clipping, transformer saturation) show on the scope and spectrum instead. It reruns after every simulation while the window is open.
    • Tubes include their interelectrode capacitances, so the Miller effect sets each stage's top corner:
    f(−3 dB, top) ≈ 1 / (2π · Rsource · (Cgk + (1 + A) · Cga))

    Loop gain. Measure → Loop gain answers "will my feedback amplifier oscillate?". The loop is broken at one end of a part, Break at: the feedback resistor, normally, and the likely ones are listed first (one from the speaker or the output transformer's secondary back into the amplifier is global feedback; one from an anode to a grid, or an op-amp's output to its inverting input, local feedback). It is broken at the end that carries more signal, the side that drives the feedback; Other end turns that round. The gain once around the loop, T, is measured by Middlebrook's double injection (a voltage injected in series at the break, then a current into it; the two give T exactly, whatever the impedances either side) and plotted as gain and phase, with 0 dB and ±180° marked.

    • Loop gain at 1 kHz, and the feedback, |1 + T|: how much the feedback lowers the gain, the output impedance and the distortion.
    • A tube amplifier's loop has two ends. At the high end the output transformer's leakage and the Miller capacitances turn the phase; at the low end the coupling capacitors and the transformer's primary inductance. At each end: where |T| falls to 1 (unity gain), the phase margin there (180° less the phase) and, where the phase reaches ±180°, the gain margin (how far |T| is below 1).
    • The verdict under the table: 45° or more of phase margin is stable (60° for no overshoot); 20–45° is stable but the response peaks and square waves ring; under 20° another speaker or a weaker tube can tip it over; below 0° it oscillates (at the low end, motorboating). A phase near 180° in the middle of the band is positive feedback: the secondary's leads are the wrong way round (or the loop is broken the wrong way round: tick Other end).
    • The generators are quiet, shorts behind their output resistance; everything else is as for the frequency response.
    Try: example 06 in Loop gain mode: about 4 dB of loop gain and 8 dB of feedback, unity gain near 37 Hz and 122 kHz with 46° of margin at the top. Make R5 (the feedback resistor) smaller to add feedback and watch the high-end margin shrink.

    11.1 Footprints

    Every part gets a through-hole footprint chosen from its values: resistors by power (10.16 mm at ¼ W to 50.8 mm at 20 W); film, ceramic and mica capacitors by value; radial, snap-in and axial electrolytics by size; DO-35 to DO-201 diodes; 3 and 5 mm LEDs; TO-92 to TO-264 transistors with each part's own pin order (TO-3 gets wire pads for a chassis heatsink); 9/16/24 mm pots and a 3296W trimmer; real tube sockets (B9A, B7G, Magnoval, octal, UX4) drawn from the component side, dual tubes sharing one socket; EI30–EI96 board transformers; toggle switches, screw terminals, RCA and jack sockets; op-amps in DIP-8 and DIP-14, a dual's or quad's amplifiers sharing one package. Chassis parts can get wire pads or a terminal instead. Change any footprint in the inspector.

    SMD. The small parts have surface-mount versions too: tick SMD in the inspector for one part, or for a whole selection (a drag box round the board, or Shift+click: every part in it that has one switches). Resistors become 0603–2512 chips by power (¼ W: 1206; above 1 W they stay through-hole), capacitors 0603–2220 chips by value, electrolytics V-chip cans up to Ø18 mm, signal diodes SOD-123 / SOD-323 / MiniMELF, rectifiers and zeners SMA / SMB / SMC (3 A types SMC), LEDs 0805 / 1206, small transistors SOT-23 (SOT-89 for the LND150 and DN2540), TO-126 parts SOT-223 or DPAK, TO-220 parts D²PAK or DPAK, the NE555 and op-amps SOIC-8 / SOIC-14, a trimmer TC33. SMD packages use the makers' SMD pin order (SOT-23: B-E-C, G-S-D; the tabbed cases B-C-E, G-D-S with the tab on the collector or drain), so check your part's datasheet. Valves, transformers, panel pots, jacks and transistors in TO-247, TO-3P, TO-264, TO-3 or germanium cans stay through-hole. An SMD part's pads are on its side's copper only and have no hole: a track reaches them on that layer (routing from one starts there; from the other side, place a via first). The bottom side (Side, or M) puts them on the bottom copper. The Gerbers then carry solder paste layers and a placement file (-pos.csv: centre, rotation and side of each SMD part) for assembly; the 3D view and STEP show the SMD bodies; the BOM in the Circuit CAD names the package ("Resistor, SMD 1206").

    Part numbers and body size. A part whose BOM part number the app knows takes the footprint it names when the number is typed or changed (the status line says so); pick another footprint afterwards and it stays until the number changes. The inspector shows what the number says, with Use it when the footprint differs. Body (mm) gives the body's size for axial parts (length, Ø), film boxes (length, width, height) and electrolytic cans (Ø, height): from the part number when it has one, else the footprint's standard size. Type a size to set it by hand (the silkscreen and the 3D model follow), clear a field to go back. A lead thicker than the footprint's hole allows gets a hole 0.3 mm over it.

    11.2 Placing and routing

    • New parts wait in a row below the board: drag them on, or Place all on the board, which puts each part where it sits in the schematic (the schematic's extent mapped onto the board), big parts first, each in the free spot nearest its place, electrolytics 10 mm from hot parts: the input ends up on the left and the output on the right, as on the sheet. Then the parts spread into the room the board has, held near those places, each on the nearest grid place that keeps every rule. Output and power transformers and filter chokes get wire pads by default (they sit on the chassis); a board-mount EI footprint is an option in the inspector. Space rotates, M moves to the bottom side. A banner offers updates when the schematic changes; placed parts keep their spot.
    • Route X: click a pad, click corners (45° bends), end on a pad, track or via of the same net. The bend follows the mouse, as in KiCad: move off the last corner along the grid and the straight run comes first, move off at 45° and the diagonal does; once the pointer is well away the bend stays, until you bring it back near the corner. V changes layer with a via; / turns the bend the other way by hand until the next corner; Backspace takes back the last corner. Width comes from the net's class. A halo shows the clearance; it turns red and refuses the click when too close (Shift+click forces it).
    • Auto-route (toolbar): routes the open connections with the board's own grid router (below), all in the page. If you prefer Freerouting, a free autorouter (GPL-3) that runs on your own computer or another one on your network (the board goes nowhere else), tick Use Freerouting in Route ▾ → Freerouting…: it then routes first and the grid router finishes what it leaves. Finish routing and Design from scratch follow the same choice.
      • Starting it. Install Java 25 and Freerouting's freerouting.jar (or use its Docker image), then leave its API server running:
        java -jar freerouting.jar --gui.enabled=false --api_server.enabled=true --api_server.authentication.enabled=false --api_server.cors_origins=https://aerocat.tech
        cors_origins names the address this page is opened from (put your own when it is served from elsewhere); without it Freerouting answers but the browser keeps the page out. authentication.enabled=false turns off its API key. In Docker it must also listen on all addresses: add --api_server.endpoints=http://0.0.0.0:37864 and publish the port (-p 37864:37864).
      • The address. With Use Freerouting ticked, Auto-route uses Freerouting once it answers at http://127.0.0.1:37864, and the grid router alone until then (the button's tooltip says which router it will use). Route ▾ → Freerouting… sets another one (a PC or server on your network: its IP, and :port if not 37864), tests it, and shows both start commands ready to copy. On aerocat.tech the dialog also offers This site's Freerouting server: no install, but it asks you to sign in once per browser (your e-mail, then a code it sends; only allowed addresses get in), and a pass there stops after 90 s. Status ↗ in the dialog opens a page on the chosen Freerouting: up or not, its version and how long it has run, processor load, memory and free disk, and the routes this browser sent (when, which board, done or failed, how long). Your own address stays an option beside it, and the choice is remembered in each browser. When Freerouting does not answer, the dialog says why: it runs but was started without this page's address in cors_origins, something else answers there, it wants an API key, or nothing answers (the API server is off, it listens only inside Docker or on its own computer, or the browser refused the address).
      • Another computer. Browsers guard an https page's call to a plain http address on your network: Chrome, Vivaldi and other Chromium browsers ask once to allow access to the local network; allow it. If the browser refuses, an SSH tunnel makes it local: ssh -N -L 37864:127.0.0.1:37864 user@that-pc, then use the default address.
      • What it routes. The board goes out with its design rules: each net's width (its class or its DC current) and the clearance between every two nets, by voltage too; pours' nets (and GND with star grounding) are left to the pour, which fills again around the new tracks; pads of a poured net the pour does not reach then get tracks of their own in a second pass. Under a valve socket's body a track may pass its pins at their own spacing (a design rule, on by default); any other pad crowded by its footprint's neighbours first gets a short straight lead out to where the full clearance holds. SMD pads are routed on their own layer.
      • The grid router. The board's own router lays every net on a fine grid (0.25 mm) on both layers, in straight and 45° runs, with vias where it must. Each net keeps its clearance (by voltage) from pads, locked copper, holes, keep-outs and the edge. Other nets' tracks are not walls but a price: a net may first run too close to another, then every net in such a conflict is taken up and routed again while the price of crowding rises, and of the spots where nets kept fighting most of all, until no two nets are too close (negotiated congestion, "PathFinder", McMurchie and Ebeling 1995, the way most FPGA routers work). Copper already there, Freerouting's or yours, stays unless a new route has to pass where it is: only those pieces come up, and their nets are routed again round the new track. A track enters a pad by a straight run to its middle, which may pass the footprint's other pins as close as they are to each other. Grid nets of valves keep off anode and heater tracks, on both sides of the board, so the check's coupling warning does not come up. After Freerouting (when it is ticked) it runs over what is still open, round the copper there, and if connections are still open, over the whole board afresh; its result stays only if it closes connections and adds no rule message (new copper a rule message points at comes off first). On a test of 109 boards laid out 9 ways each it left 131 connections open where Freerouting with the older finishers left 736, and routed 98 of the boards completely (91 before). A big board takes up to 20 s; it runs in the background.
      • While it routes. The status line shows a bar, what runs now and the time so far. During the grid router's passes it adds the pass, how many crossings (nets too close to each other) it still has to sort out and how many connections are not joined yet, with a small graph of the crossings falling pass by pass; a thin line under the bar shows how far the passes have gone. Stop beside it ends the run at once: what is routed so far stays, and one Ctrl+Z takes it back. Without Freerouting the grid router goes first and the maze search after it, on what it left.
      • What is left after that. Each connection still open is tried on a grid: if a track of its net's width can still run from end to end within the rules past the copper already there (Lee's maze search), it is added, one at a time, and kept only when it closes the connection and adds no rule message. Then each one still open is tried exactly: a small window round it becomes a yes/no puzzle (each grid cell holds at most one connection, a used cell joins two neighbours, different nets keep their clearance) that a solver in the page answers. It may take up the unlocked tracks of that net and of the nets walling it in and lay them again, tries without vias first, then shortens each track; the status line counts what it joined (6 joined on a grid where Freerouting left a way). When it finds no way, there is none in that window without moving parts. When connections stay open, the status line names the pins that are shut in: those whose tracks cannot get out of their part's neighbourhood at all, and what walls them in (VT1 pin 2 (shut in by a track on B.Cu, R3)). That check counts all of a part's open pins at once, as a maximum flow from the pins to free space through cells one track apart on both layers.
      • Old copper. Tracks already on the board stay, except copper left over from where parts used to be (loose ends, copper reaching no pad or joining two nets, tracks breaking a clearance): that goes first, and those connections are routed afresh; the status line says how many pieces went. Locked copper always stays. What Freerouting cannot route within the rules stays in the ratsnest; one Ctrl+Z takes the whole run back.
      • By hand, without the API server: Export ▾ → Freerouting design (DSN), route it in Freerouting (its window, or java -jar freerouting.jar -de board.dsn -do board.ses), then Route ▾ → Import routes (SES)….
    • Finish routing (Route ▾; called Route & nudge before 3.131): for when Auto-route leaves connections it cannot fit, keeping your layout. It routes, then, while connections are left, moves the parts near them a little and routes again, up to 5 rounds (it stops after two in a row that do not help), then tries two rounds that spread the parts over the whole board; a round that does not help is undone.
      • Which parts move. Unlocked parts within reach of an open connection (8 mm, widening each round), and, wherever they are, the parts that wall in a shut-in pin or block the way between an open connection's ends. Locked parts never move, and neither do valves, transformers, pots, switches, connectors, fuses or anything bigger than about 35 × 35 mm. Lock a part (inspector) to keep it exactly where it is.
      • A shut-in pin first. Each round it first looks for the smallest shift (up to 5 mm) or quarter turn of the shut-in part, or of a part walling it in, after which the pin gets out and a track can run on to the other end (with the copper in the way taken up). If it finds one, that is the round's move.
      • How it chooses. It tries thousands of small moves on the 1.27 mm grid (a shift, a quarter turn, or two parts of the same footprint swapped) and keeps the layout that scores best on the shortest wires, the fewest ratsnest crossings, the least crowding (the wire each net needs against the room for tracks round it) and the smallest moves (simulated annealing, as chip placers do). Every move keeps the rules: inside the outline, courtyards apart, clear of mounting holes and part keep-outs, electrolytics away from hot parts, and pads their clearance (by voltage) from other nets.
      • Then it routes. The unlocked copper of the moved parts' nets and of the open connections, any passing near those connections, and the tracks and vias that wall them in (even when no part can move) are taken up; Freerouting routes the nets that failed first, then the rest.
      • Last, the whole board. If connections are still open, the parts that may move are spread over the whole board, two ways: once for the shortest wiring wherever there is room, once held near where they are. A part with a shut-in pin counts as twice its size, and one at the end of an open connection as one and a half times, so each gets room round its pins (analytic placement as chip and board placers do it: wire length against the electrostatic energy of the parts' area). Each part then goes to the nearest grid place that keeps every rule, keeping its turn; every unlocked track comes up and Freerouting routes the board afresh. The result is kept only if it is better.
      • Afterwards the status line lists what moved and how far (R7 2.5 mm, C3 turned 90°); one Ctrl+Z takes the whole run back. While it runs, the Auto-route button reads Stop: it stops after the pass under way and keeps the best layout so far. Each round is a Freerouting pass, so a big board takes a few minutes; the window keeps answering meanwhile (the heaviest step runs in the background). While the parts are spread over the board you see that layout; if it routes no better (more connections open, or as many with longer tracks) the earlier one comes back, and the status line says so.
    • Design from scratch (Route ▾; called Rearrange board before 3.131): an automatic layout of the whole board. Every unlocked part comes off and goes back, valves and transformers too; pots, switches, jacks, connectors and fuses stay where the panel needs them (those still waiting below a fresh board go on it first, where they sit in the schematic), and locked parts and locked copper stay as they are. The outline, edge clearance, mounting holes and part keep-outs hold throughout.
      • Nine layouts. Each starts with the parts where they sit in the schematic (as Place all does) and spreads them over the board, one of several ways: held near those places, for the shortest wiring, with more room round each part, or a little more room. A valve counts as 1.6 times its size and a part with a high-voltage pin 1.2 times, so their wide clearances get room.
      • Parts turn. While the parts spread, each one in turn tries its four quarter turns and leans to the one that gives its nets the shortest wiring and the fewest crossings with other nets (as the Cypress board placer does, ISPD 2025); one layout keeps the turns as they are, for comparison. Then thousands of small moves (a shift, a quarter turn, two alike parts swapped) polish the layout.
      • Scored before routing. Each layout gets a score without routing it: 40 for a pin that cannot get a track out at all, 4 for each crossing of two nets' ratsnest lines (12 when one is a high-voltage net), 1 for each mm of ratsnest. The best layout of each way, three in all, is routed by Freerouting and the grid finishers; the best routed one stays (fewest open connections, then rule messages, then track length), the board as it was included. If connections are still open, Finish routing's rounds work on them.
      • Time. On a three-valve board it takes several minutes, most of it Freerouting. The layouts are made in the background, so the window keeps answering; the status line shows a progress bar with the step, what it is doing and the time so far. The Auto-route button reads Stop: it stops after the pass under way and keeps the best so far. One Ctrl+Z takes it all back.
    • Stitching vias: a pad of a poured net (GND) that the pour cannot reach, shut in by other copper or by a high-voltage pin's wide clearance, gets a short track on the other layer and a via down into the pour. Auto-route, Finish routing and Design from scratch do this by themselves after the grid finishers: the nearest via spots are tried first, and a via stays only if it joins the pad without a new rule violation.
    • Tidy tracks (automatic): routing pulls the unlocked tracks tight. A track the grid finishers laid steps along their grid, so a diagonal run comes out as a staircase; tidying turns staircases and detours into straight and 45° runs wherever that keeps every clearance (never closer to other copper than the rules, or than the old track already was). Points where other copper of the net joins stay. First, doubled copper comes off: a track laid beside one of its own net that already joins the same pads (left when a route was laid again next to an old one). Auto-route, Finish routing and Design from scratch do this by themselves after each pass; on a board with nothing left to route, Auto-route just tidies it.
    • Unroute (Route ▾): removes every track and via from the board, to route again from scratch; locked tracks and vias stay. One step: Ctrl+Z brings them back.
    • Edit tracks: drag a segment sideways (45° corners stay 45°). U selects the whole connected track. Deleting one track of a route takes the copper of that route it leaves hanging (stubs and vias touching nothing) with it, up to the pads, a pour or copper still in use; locked copper stays, and Ctrl+Z brings it all back.
    • Several at once: box-select left-to-right (inside) or right-to-left (touching); Shift+click adds. Arrows nudge (Shift: ×10). Align and distribute in the inspector. L locks.
    • Copper pours (P: click the corners, double-click to close; or from the board inspector + GND plane (bottom) and + GND pour round the parts; or, with several parts selected, + GND pour round these parts). A pour keeps every other net's clearance (by voltage too), joins its own net's pads by thermal reliefs and drops islands, so it never shorts anything. It is a shape you edit: select it (click its outline, or the poured copper itself where no part, track or via lies under the pointer; dragging from empty space still draws a selection box), drag a corner to move it, drag the + in the middle of an edge to add a corner, double-click a corner (or drop it onto its neighbour) to remove it, drag its outline or inside it to move it all; the copper refills as you drag. Round the parts and Board rectangle in its inspector reshape it at once. The board outline cuts it, round corners and edge clearance included. Del, or right-click → Delete, removes the selected pour.
    • Keep-out areas (K): forbid tracks, vias, pours and optionally parts on either layer (under a mains transformer, along heater wiring).
    • Measure (D) snaps to pad centres, track corners, board corners and edges.
    • Nets tab: every net's class, DC current, routing left and length; click one to highlight it.
    • Cross-probing: selecting a part on the board selects it in the schematic and back. Nets too: highlighting a net on the board (the Nets tab or `) selects its wires in the schematic, and wires of one net selected in the schematic light that net up on the board until the selection is cleared.
    • Comfort: rest the pointer on a pad, part, track or via for a card with its net, class, voltage and current; while routing the card shows the net, width, layer and length, and W steps the width. Right-click opens a menu for what is under the pointer (turn, flip, lock, route from a pad, a track's width or layer, a pour's shape, a via or hole right there); right-drag still pans. + / − and the zoom bar zoom, Z zooms to the selection, Ctrl+F finds a part or net, \ hides the side panels. The checks and the layers fold away and stay folded; long check lists are grouped by kind. Values sit inside a part when they fit and never cover a reference.

    11.3 Design rules and checks

    Board ▾ → Design rules… sets clearances (with a separate high-voltage clearance above a threshold voltage), the edge clearance, track widths, via and drill sizes, annular ring, solder-mask opening, and the allowed warming of a track at its DC current. A new board starts with 0.6 mm signal tracks, 1 mm power and high-voltage tracks (wider where a net's DC current asks), 0.4 mm clearance and 2 mm high-voltage clearance, so the autorouter can get tracks out of crowded pins such as a TO-92's or a DIP's. A board saved with other rules keeps them; if Auto-route leaves connections at small parts' pins, try narrower tracks or less clearance here before moving parts. Net classes (Signal, Power, HV) are set automatically from the simulated DC voltage, or by hand; heater nets are Power.

    Clearance by voltage. On top of the classes, two nets keep the gap IPC-2221B table 6-1 asks for the largest voltage between them. The voltage is the worst case of their simulated DC levels and their swing in the simulation; the anode end of an output transformer's primary counts at twice its DC level, which is where an inductive load takes it at full drive. Pick the table for the board: B2 bare board (0.6 mm to 150 V, 1.25 mm to 300 V, 2.5 mm to 500 V, then 0.005 mm per volt), B4 under a permanent coating, A6 conformally coated, or Off. A mounting hole counts as chassis, 0 V. Routing, pours and the check all use it; the net table's To GND column shows each net's clearance to 0 V copper. Valve socket pins sit closer together than any HV clearance, so two exceptions apply: a track's segment that ends in a pin may pass the footprint's other pins as close as that pin is to them, and (Under a socket's body, tracks may pass its pins at their own spacing, on by default) anywhere under the socket's body a track or via may pass a pin as close as the pin's nearest neighbour is; the socket fixes that spacing anyway.

    Heaters and shields. The schematic draws a valve's anode, grids and cathode; the board also wires its heater. Every indirectly heated valve gets its heater pins on heater nets, one pair per heater voltage (HTR6.3_A, HTR6.3_B), fed from a heater-supply connector XH1 that carries the sum of the heater currents, so the current check sizes the heater tracks. 12AX7-type valves run at 6.3 V (pins 4 + 5 against 9) or, if chosen, 12.6 V (4 to 5); a heater raised to a DC potential (the tube's Heater DC potential) gets its own supply at that voltage; a heater joined to its cathode (GZ34) gets a winding of its own. Directly heated valves are left alone: their filament is the cathode. Internal shield pins (6N23P pin 9) go to GND. Both can be turned off.

    Mounting holes (H, or Board setup): Ø 3.2 mm for M3 by default. In the inspector, Plating makes one plated: a copper ring on both sides (Ring Ø, 6 mm by default), bare of solder mask, drilled with the plated holes. Give a plated hole a Net (GND for a chassis screw that grounds the board) and a pour of that net joins it; the check reports one its net does not reach. A hole that is not plated keeps every net's chassis clearance.

    A part's own holes. Some footprints carry unplated holes of their own, for the plastic pegs that locate a part (the Neutrik NJ2FD-V, NJ3FD-V and NJ5FD-V vertical jacks have two, Ø 1.6 and Ø 2.1 mm). They move and turn with the part, show with the mounting holes (the dashed ring is the copper clearance), go into the NPTH drill file as component holes with the mask opened over them, and are drilled through the 3D board. A peg is not a screw, so tracks, pours and the check keep only the net class's clearance from it, not the chassis clearance.

    The design-rule check runs live: clearances, shorts, edge, annular rings and drills, widths, track width for its simulated DC current per IPC-2221, overlapping parts, mounting holes under parts (the screw head, washer and standoff need Ø 7 mm free round an M3 hole), keep-outs, silkscreen over pads, dangling tracks and vias, unplaced parts, pinouts to verify. Click an entry to go to it. Errors are red; warnings amber; the status bar turns green only when nothing is left to route.

    Net names say what a net is: a sheet connector's name; B+ (or B+1, B+2 … from the highest voltage down) for supply rails, +12V for low-voltage ones; a valve electrode as VL1.1-A, VL2-G1, VL1.2-K; OUT for the output transformer's secondary, IN for a generator's output; otherwise the parts it joins (R8_C3). Boards saved with the older Net-(R8-2) names move to the new ones by themselves.

    Star grounding (Ground scheme: Star point). A star terminal XG1 (a plated M3 or M4 hole for a solder lug) joins every ground return, and nothing else may. Each part with a ground pad belongs to a return: Supply (sources, rectifiers, reservoir and filter capacitors, bleeders), Output (power valves, the output transformer's secondary, the speaker), Input (the input jack), or the small-signal stage it serves (VL1: its cathode resistor, bypass and grid leak). A part's inspector shows its return and can move it. The ratsnest joins each return's pads and runs the return to XG1 on its own; ground copper that joins two returns anywhere else is reported, since the reservoir's charging pulses would then flow through the signal ground. A ground pour joins everything, so it is for the Free scheme.

    Teardrops (on by default): where a track meets a round pad or a via, copper widens from the track to most of the pad's width over about a pad's length, so the joint survives drilling slop, soldering heat and a heavy part's leverage. They go to the Gerbers, PDFs and SVG; one is left out where it would break a clearance, and pours keep clear of them. A track as wide as its pad needs none.

    Valve layout checks (warnings, each with its distance in the rules):

    • Grid stopper at the socket: a resistor that is the only part on a grid net, further from its grid pin than 10 mm past the socket's body (on a big octal socket a pin sits well inside the body, so the stopper may be that much further). Design from scratch pulls each stopper to its socket. The track before the stopper is an antenna.
    • Grid tracks near anodes and heaters: a grid track running alongside an anode track (oscillation) or a heater track (hum) for more than 10 mm, closer than 2 mm, on either side of the board.
    • Electrolytics near hot parts: closer than 10 mm to a power valve, a rectifier or a resistor dissipating 1 W or more in the simulation.
    • Mounting hole near each socket: a board-mounted valve socket with no mounting hole within 40 mm; pulling a valve flexes the board.

    14. Design cookbook

    Worked procedures, with the formulas the simulator lets you check.

    14.1 A triode voltage amplifier

    1. Pick Va and Ia from the curve tracer: a 12AX7 at 150–200 V and 0.8–1.2 mA; a 6N2P similar; a 12AU7 / 6N8S at 8–10 mA.
    2. Plate resistor: Ra = (B+ − Va) / Ia. 100–220 kΩ for a 12AX7.
    3. Cathode resistor: Rk = −Vg / Ia (read Vg at the operating point on the curves),; try the nearest E24 values and read Ia in the tube's inspector.
    4. Gain, bypassed: A = µ · Ra / (Ra + rp). Unbypassed: A = µ · Ra / (Ra + rp + (µ + 1) · Rk). Compare with the inspector's Plate/grid gain.
    5. Cathode bypass corner: f ≈ 1 / (2π · Ck · (Rk ‖ (Ra + rp)/(µ + 1))).
    6. Coupling capacitor corner: f = 1 / (2π · Cc · (Rout + Rnext)). Check both on the Bode plot.
    7. Top corner (Miller): see Bode plotter.

    14.2 A single-ended output stage

    1. Choose the load: roughly Zp ≈ 2 · Va / Ia for a pentode, 2–3 × rp for a triode.
    2. Bias for 60–70 % of Pa max: adjust the cathode resistor (or the bias voltage) and read Pa in the tube's inspector against Pa max.
    3. Check the output transformer's DC current rating (the check does it) and its primary inductance for the bass: f−3dB ≈ (rp ‖ Zp) / (2π · Lp).
    4. Drive it to just below clipping: watch the scope for flat tops and the spectrum's THD. Read Output (speakers) in the circuit summary.
    5. Check Pg2 at full drive (pentodes): it is highest there.

    14.3 A push-pull stage

    • Use the push-pull OPT; drive the grids 180° apart (a phase splitter, or two generators at 0° and 180°).
    • Ultralinear: screens to the UL taps (about 40 % for KT88/EL34, 20–25 % elsewhere).
    • Fixed bias: one bias pot per tube on a negative line; check with the pot at both ends that it covers tubes drawing ±30 % (a fresh, unmatched set).
    • The spectrum should show H2 cancelled and H3 dominant; a large H2 means unbalance.

    14.4 A power supply

    • Mains → power transformer → rectifier (GZ34, 5U4G, 5Y3GT, EZ81, 6X4, or 1N4007s) → reservoir C → choke or resistor → filter C.
    • Capacitor-input DC ≈ 1.2–1.4 × Vrms at load. Ripple ≈ Idc / (2 · f · C) peak-to-peak for full-wave.
    • Check the rectifier's current and PIV, the capacitors' peak voltages (BOM) and the resistor dissipation.
    • Watch the power-on transient on the scope for the turn-on surge and voltage overshoot.

    14.5 Global feedback

    • Take a resistor from the speaker to the cathode of the first stage (unbypassed part of Rk).
    • Check stability on the Bode plot: no peak above the 1 kHz gain, and phase not reaching −180° where the loop gain is still above 1. A square wave on the scope should show little ringing.
    • Feedback reduces gain, THD and output impedance by about the same factor (1 + loop gain).

    15. How the simulator works

    • Solver. Modified nodal analysis. Newton–Raphson with gmin and source stepping for the DC operating point; transient with Gear's second-order method (BDF2), which keeps resonances at their true Q without trapezoidal ringing.
    • Steady state by shooting. The steady state is the state that one period maps onto itself; Newton–GMRES finds it, so slow coupling, cathode and filter capacitors settle in a few dozen periods instead of hundreds. If it does not converge in time, plain settling continues, extrapolating the slowly decaying response (minimal polynomial extrapolation).
    • Sparse solves. A sparse LU (Gilbert–Peierls, as KLU) whose numeric part runs as WebAssembly; work grows about linearly with the circuit.
    • Triodes: Koren's published equation unmodified:
    E1 = (Va / kp) · ln(1 + exp(kp · (1/µ + Vg / √(kvb + Va²))))
Ia = 2 · E1^x / kg1
    • Pentodes: Koren's space-charge term, divided between plate and screen with a separate knee and slope; what the plate loses in the knee goes to the screen:
    Is  = 2 · E1^x / kg1      with E1 = (Vg2 / kp) · ln(1 + exp(kp · (1/µ + Vg / Vg2)))
Ia  = Is · tanh(Va / vk) · (1 + Va / λ)
Ig2 = (Vg + Vg2/µ)^x / kg2 + ks · Is · (1 − tanh(Va / vk))
    • Grid conduction is included; below 2 V on the screen the currents fade smoothly to zero.
    • Interelectrode capacitances: Cgk, Cga, Cak (triode); Cin, Cg1a, Cout (pentode), from the datasheets, a Soviet tube taking its Western analog's, else typical values.
    • AC analysis: every device linearised at the operating point (its Jacobian by central differences of the same equations); the complex system G + jωC solved at 40 frequencies per decade.
    • Rectifier tubes: vacuum diodes (Child–Langmuir), perveance from datasheet drops.
    • Transistors: SPICE Gummel–Poon (IS, BF, BR, VAF, IKF) with junction capacitances, and base and emitter resistance (RB, RE) for the drivers and power parts (and BD139 / BD140), fitted with IS to the datasheet's typical VBE curve, so their VBE rises with the current as a real part's does; the inspector's VBE and VCE are read at the pins. MOSFETs: square law (level 1) with a smoothed threshold, body diode and gate capacitances. Diodes/zeners/LEDs: exponential junctions; diodes with SPICE's stored charge (TT: the reverse recovery, which agrees with ngspice's to a few per cent) and junction capacitance. Op-amps: a two-stage macromodel, the input stage's current limited (slew rate), a Miller capacitor across the second stage (gain-bandwidth), the output within the datasheet's distance of each rail; for the DC operating point the loop gain is raised in steps when the full gain does not converge at once. JFETs: SPICE's JFET (the square law, KP = 2·BETA) with a gate junction to the drain and to the source; checked against ngspice's own JFET to 0.05 %. Regulators and the TL431: behavioural: an error amplifier of the datasheet's gain that can only source (or, the TL431, only sink), its current limited, the pass device fading out below the dropout; their gain is stepped up for the DC operating point as an op-amp's.
    • Transformers: coupled inductors with leakage; an output transformer's core saturates (its flux linkage λ against the magnetising current, linear up to a knee that bends over with sharpness 4, then only 1 % of the inductance left), its leakage on each winding. Loudspeakers: Re + Le in series with a parallel R‖L‖C motional impedance at fs.
    • It runs in Web Workers, one per processor core, so the editor stays responsive and the calculations run side by side: the live simulation, the power-on transient and the Bode sweep each on a core of their own, tolerance runs on all of them, and the Bode sweep's frequencies split across the free cores. One steady-state run is a time-stepped solution, each step built on the one before, so it uses one core.

    16. Accuracy and limits

    • The engine is tested against closed-form results (dividers, RC/RL, resonance), an independent RK4 integration of a choke-input supply (0.02 %), Kirchhoff's law at every node, and the frequency response against RC corners and the Miller estimate.
    • Every amplifier tube is fitted to and tested against its datasheet operating points; fitted currents are within a few percent.
    • 76 reference circuits (RCA, Philips, GE datasheet circuits; textbook stages) are measured through the instruments as a user would.
    Know the limits.
    • A model is exact only near its fit points, and production tubes vary ±20 % or more.
    • The 12AX7's µ rises to about 106 at 150–170 V (fitted at 250 V), so small-signal gains there come out 5–20 % high.
    • The knee of pentodes without published full-power data (EL34, KT88, 6P1P, GU-50, 6P45S) is a median of other fits.
    • Heaters are not simulated (the cathode is always hot); there is no warm-up.
    • Transistor models leave out base resistance and thermal effects: solid-state distortion comes out optimistic.

    17. Troubleshooting and FAQ

    The instruments say "Waiting for the Circuit CAD…"

    They need the Circuit CAD window open in the same browser. Open them from its Windows ▾ menu; the Schematic | Board switch at the left of their toolbar (or Alt+S and Alt+B) brings up the CAD, opened if it is closed, or the board (in the CAD window when Board in this window is ticked there).

    An instrument says "no scope in circuit"

    Place an Oscilloscope part (palette → Instruments) and wire its channels.

    The Bode plotter shows nothing

    It needs a signal generator in the circuit as well as a scope part.

    A voltage sticker is missing

    There was no free room for it. Zoom in, or hover the wire to read it in the status bar.

    The gain is higher than my hand calculation

    Check rp and µ at your operating point in the curve tracer (markers give gm, rp, µ at a point): they change with current. See the 12AX7 note in Accuracy.

    My real amp draws a different current

    Normal tube spread: real tubes of one type differ by ±30 % in anode current at the same bias. Adjust the cathode resistor (or the bias voltage) in the circuit until the simulation draws what you measured.

    Where are my circuits kept?

    The open circuit is autosaved in this browser. Save files (Ctrl+S) to keep them safely or move them between computers.

    Can I run it offline?

    Yes: any static web server, e.g. python3 -m http.server in the folder. The simulator needs http://, not file://.

    • With each question the circuit described above goes to the provider; the How-to questions also send this manual. Nothing is sent until you ask.
    • The provider bills your account for what you ask. A question about a large circuit with the manual is about 30 000 tokens in; Claude keeps the manual in its prompt cache between questions, which makes the next ones cheaper.
    • The defaults are each provider's strongest everyday model (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro). The cheaper ones (Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Luna, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite) answer faster and are fine for questions about the app.

    18.2 Ask, Explain

    Type a question in the box at the bottom of the AI tab and press Enter (Shift+Enter starts a new line): "why does VL2 run so hot?", "what does C3 do?", "how do I measure the power at 1 % THD?". The conversation keeps its last dozen turns, so you can follow up; Clear starts a new one. Explain my circuit (or Explain circuit in the tab) asks for a stage-by-stage reading: what the circuit is, what each part is for and why it has its value, how each stage is biased (from the simulation) and what looks unusual. The answer appears while it is written; Stop abandons a question that takes too long.

    18.3 Fix the checks

    Fix the checks turns the Checks list (parts beyond their ratings, a muted chip amplifier, a regulator without headroom) into value changes: a table of parts with the value now, the proposed one and why. Each change is checked against the part before it is offered (the part exists, has that value, and the value is one it can take: a resistor's power rating from its list, a tube from the library); one that fails says why and has no button. Apply (or Apply all) makes the changes as one undo step, simulates the circuit again and reports how the Checks changed ("Checks: 6 → 0 problems. All clear."), with an Undo button (it takes back that change only; after other edits use Edit ▾ → Undo). The simulator, not the model, has the last word: read the new operating point before you keep a change.

    18.4 Auto-tune

    The Tune tab under the schematic (AI ▾ → Auto-tune) changes part values until the simulated circuit meets your goals. It needs no API key: an optimizer (a pattern search) proposes values, the simulator runs each try on all its workers, and the tries that get closer to the goals are kept.

    • Goals: + Goal… lists everything the CAD measures (each tube's and transistor's current, voltages and dissipation, each stage's gain, each speaker's output power and THD, each scope channel's THD). Set ≥, ≤ or = and a target: "BA1 power ≥ 10 W", "BA1 THD ≤ 1 %", "VL2 anode current = 48 mA". Keep every tube and transistor within its ratings counts a part past its ratings as a missed goal. With no goal yet, one button adds the usual pair: the speaker's power a quarter above what it gives now, at no more than 1 % THD.
    • Values to change: + Value… lists every editable value of every part (resistances, capacitances, the generator's level, a supply's voltage); Suggest adds the resistors at the tubes' and transistors' pins. Give each a range; resistors and capacitors are searched on a log scale. E24/E12 rounds the result to standard values.
    • Tries sets the budget (120 by default). ▶ Tune starts and ■ Stop keeps the best found. The line above the chart reads like "Try 17 / 120 · 1 of 2 goals met · best at try 9 · off by 4.2 %". The chart has the try number across and, up the side, how far the measured values are off the goals (the rms of each goal's miss in %, on a log scale; a part past its ratings adds to it): a grey dot per try (× for a try that did not settle), the cyan line for the best so far, a yellow dot at the best try, a dotted line at the try running now, and the green band at the foot where every goal is met (0). Hover a dot for that try. The goals table's Off by column says how far each goal is from its target. At the end the best values are rounded to their series and simulated once more, and the table shows each goal at the start and at the result.
    • Apply these values puts them on the sheet as one undo step. If the circuit changes after a run, the result is marked as out of date. Undo after Apply brings back the button, so the values can be set again; Redo shows them as applied.
    • AI start (with an API key) asks the model which values to change for your goals, over what ranges and from where; it sets them in the tab and the starting values on the sheet (Ctrl+Z takes them back). The optimizer and the simulator still do the tuning.

    18.5 Design from a description

    AI ▾ → Design from a description… asks the model for a circuit: describe the amplifier or stage (tubes, output power, load, supply voltage, gain, what matters to you) or start from one of the examples, and press Design (Ctrl+Enter). It needs your API key.

    • The model answers with a netlist of the library's parts: each part with its values and the net each pin is on, the supplies as ideal rails, a signal generator at the input, the load at the output and a scope on both. The CAD checks it against the library (the part types, pins and values exist); a design it cannot use goes back once with the problems.
    • The CAD draws it on a new sheet, in signal order from left to right. Every pin gets a short stub ending in a ground, a supply rail or a sheet connector named after its net (connectors of one name are one net), so the drawing is correct before it is tidy: drag parts and draw wires to make it your own.
    • The circuit is simulated. With Send the Checks back ticked, problems in the Checks go to the model with the measured operating points for up to two revisions. The AI tab shows the design's summary and the Checks at the end.
    • It replaces the circuit on the sheet; Ctrl+Z steps back through the revisions to the circuit you had. Then Fix the checks or Auto-tune can take it further.

    18.6 Tube from a datasheet

    AI ▾ → Tube from a datasheet… turns a tube's datasheet (a PDF, up to about 30 MB) into a tube in the library. Pick the file, optionally type the tube's name, and press Read datasheet. It needs your API key; the PDF goes to your provider.

    • The model reads the type, socket, heater, ratings (Va, Pa, Ik, and for a pentode Vg2 and Pg2), the pinout and points along the plate curves (for a pentode at one screen voltage).
    • A Koren model is fitted to the points, starting from the most similar tube in the library (the same fitter as the curve tracer's). A pentode's knee and screen terms come from that tube. Too few points: the similar tube's model is used and the review says so.
    • The review in the AI tab lists what was read, the fit (R² and the RMS error in mA) and a chart of the model's curves over the datasheet's points. Check the pinout and ratings against the datasheet: they were read by the AI.
    • Add to the library makes it a custom tube, like an imported SPICE model: it is kept in this browser, saved inside the circuit files that use it, and listed with a Remove button under the tube inspector's Import SPICE model…. A name that is already a built-in tube gets "-DS" added.
